One line at the foot of the canvas

The grammar names lift-to-bin as the canvas's one bulk act and then gives the bin nowhere to be. It is the last canvas-level surface, and it should be the smallest thing in the product: one line, at the end of the order, which is absent when nothing has been lifted. Not a view — the views are gone. Not a block — nothing in it is being worked on. Not a modal, because a person opening the bin is comparing it to the canvas and a modal would hide the thing they are comparing against.

CS3 already wrote the only copy this surface needs

"Lifted shots and unused panels. Nothing here is in the film; nothing here is lost." Two clauses that answer both questions a person has, in the order they have them. It carries forward verbatim — the only change is that it now says Pieces rather than shots and panels.

§1
Why the foot, and not a rail tab or a corner button

The canvas is dependency-ordered, and the bin is the end of that order

A lifted Piece is precisely a thing with no dependents and nothing depending on it. Putting it at the foot is not filing — it is where the existing ordering rule already puts it. Nobody has to learn a location.

Not in the rail

The rail is where things that cost money or need wording are said. Nothing about the bin costs anything, and it holds no decision. A tab there would be the first permanent chrome in the product that is only ever about housekeeping.

Absent, not empty

Until something is lifted, the line does not exist. The grammar's rule that there is no empty state applies here more than anywhere: a bin that announces its own emptiness is an invitation to throw something away.

It expands in place, and stays open

Clicking the line expands it above itself into a single row of members — never a second column, never a drawer over the canvas. Open or closed is remembered per project, like a run's collapse, because a person who is putting things back does it several times in a row.

§2
Drawn — closed, open, and on a dark canvas

Closed. The default. Grey, quiet, and the width of the canvas — it reads as the floor of the page rather than an object on it.

Put back is the only action, and it is free

A Piece returns to its place in dependency order — not to the end. It was never moved; it was hidden. There is no Put all back: four is the realistic count, and a bulk restore would be the second bulk act in a product that permits one.

Statuses are not shown here

An item keeps its status and does not display it. A stale clip in the bin is not asking anyone for anything, and the density invariant is about blocks that need a person — nothing in the bin does, by definition.

§3
What may be in it, and what may never

MAY BE LIFTED
AND WHY THE OTHERS CANNOT

A whole block, or one member of a set
Both, and they look the same in the bin — a lifted member is a Piece, and the grammar has always said a photograph is a stills set of one. Lifting the last member of a set lifts the set; a block with no members cannot exist.

An unused panel — a shot that was planned and never produced
CS3's word, kept. It carries its script line as its face, exactly as an unproduced board member does. Nothing was spent on it and it still is not nothing — someone wrote it.

Never a PROPOSED block
Lifting a proposal is Discard, and Discard already exists in the judgement footer. Two verbs for one act would put the decision in two homes, which is the thing proposal-as-status was chosen to prevent. A declined proposal leaves no trace, because nothing was made.

Never a NEEDS BUDGET block
Same shape: declining a quote removes the block, per the quote rule. An unanswered quote in a bin would be a cost sitting somewhere a person does not look, which is the one thing the quote rules exist to prevent.

Never the script
Everything is downstream of it. Lifting it would stale the entire canvas and leave no way to read what the work was for. The document kind has no lift, and its absence needs no explanation on screen.

Never something the film is using
A clip referenced by the cut cannot be lifted while it is in the film — lifting it would silently shorten the film. It becomes liftable once the Director has cut it out, which is speech, which is where structural changes belong.

§4
The arithmetic, which is where this would otherwise go wrong

Bin items count toward 96. They do not count toward density.

They were made and they cost money, so hiding them from the ceiling would let a person walk into it without warning — and would make lifting a way to buy more room, which it is not. But they are not on the canvas, so they do not push it from the default step into quiet. Both halves are one sentence in implementation and both are easy to get backwards.

The count reads "14 of 96 pieces · 4 in the bin"

Plain grey, no meter, as the Runs answer specified. The bin's own count is stated beside it rather than folded in, because a person near the ceiling needs to know that four of their pieces are recoverable — that is the one moment the bin is genuinely useful.

Lifting closes a run rather than splitting it

A run derives from adjacency, and a lifted Piece leaves canvas order entirely — so its neighbours become adjacent and the run stays whole, at thirteen attempts instead of fourteen. This is the opposite of the edge the Runs answer flagged, where a new Piece between two attempts breaks a run in half. Removal heals; insertion divides. Both are honest, and neither needed a new rule.

Putting back does not re-run anything

A restored Piece returns with the status it left with, including STALE. Nothing is re-generated, nothing is re-priced, and no chain is rewritten — the film did not change while it was gone, because it could not have been in the film to begin with.

§5
One thing here is not a design question

Nothing in this product deletes, and eventually something must

"Nothing is lost" is a promise about a person's work, not a storage policy. Frames accumulate, projects are abandoned, and someone will eventually ask to have a face removed. That is an owner ruling, not a surface — and the bin is where its consequence would land, so it is worth deciding before Pilot rather than after. If permanent removal ever exists, my recommendation is that it lives in project settings and never on this line: a person tidying a canvas should not be one click from destroying something.

Two decisions here to argue with

1. Bin items count toward the 96 ceiling. It is the honest reading and it will feel unfair to someone who has tidied up.2. No Put all back. If Pilot shows people lifting in batches, this is the first thing I would add — and it would be the second bulk act, so it needs saying out loud.

What this closes

The grammar's last dangling rule. Lift-to-bin now has a destination, the canvas has exactly one canvas-level surface, and no kind gained a row. Phase 6's remaining open items are all rulings, not drawings.
